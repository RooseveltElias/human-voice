# Human Voice Writing Skill

This skill encodes writing principles from two sources: pre-2021 peer-reviewed
scholarship on what makes prose clear, credible, and human, and a continuously
updated detection catalogue of AI writing patterns. The scraper in
`scripts/update_patterns.py` keeps the detection side current. The academic
side is stable and lives here.

Read the Quick-Scan Checklist before producing or revising any text. For
detailed guidance on any section, follow the references listed at the bottom.


## Quick-Scan Checklist

Work through this before finishing any piece of writing.

WORD LEVEL
- Banned words removed? (see Banned Words below)
- Long words replaced with short ones where meaning is the same?
- Nominalizations converted to verbs? ("made a decision" becomes "decided")
- Passive voice removed where active works better?
- Dead metaphors cut or replaced with fresh images?

SENTENCE LEVEL
- Sentence length varies aggressively? Mix short and long throughout.
- Each sentence begins with the character or agent performing the action?
- Participial phrases kept rare? (the comma + -ing construction)
- No more than one or two uses of "not" per paragraph?
- Old information comes before new in each sentence?

STRUCTURE LEVEL
- No "In conclusion", "Overall", "In summary", "To summarize"?
- No summary of what was just said unless the document genuinely needs one?
- Opening is the most interesting sentence, not context-setting?
- Paragraphs vary in length and shape?
- No formulaic three-part structure used by default?

TONE LEVEL
- A position is stated and held, not hedged into mush?
- No "it is important to note that", "generally speaking", "to some extent"?
- No "while there are benefits, there are also drawbacks" balance reflex?
- Specific facts and details present, not only abstract claims?
- Tonal variation present? Urgency where urgent, dry where dry, direct always?

PUNCTUATION
- No em dash used where a comma, colon, or full stop works?
- En dashes used for ranges (1990-2000, not 1990-2000)?
- No excessive bolding of terms mid-prose?
- No emoji in headers or body copy?


## The Academic Foundation

This section distills the most useful writing principles from pre-2021
scholarship. These are not preferences. They are the result of decades of
research into what makes prose clear, credible, and readable.


### Orwell (1946): Six Rules for Clear Writing

George Orwell spent his 1946 essay "Politics and the English Language"
cataloguing what destroys prose: dying metaphors, verbal false limbs, pretentious
diction, and meaningless words. His six rules follow from that diagnosis.

Rule 1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech which you are used
to seeing in print. Worn-out phrases like "iron resolution", "hotbed",
"paradigm shift", or "game-changer" do not conjure images. They anesthetize.
Use original images or none at all.

Rule 2. Never use a long word where a short one will do. Prefer Saxon words
over Latin ones. "Help" over "facilitate". "Show" over "demonstrate". "Use"
over "utilize". "Tell" over "communicate". Latin and Greek roots inflate
sentences without adding meaning.

Rule 3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. Every word
that does no work weakens the words that do. Cut "in order to" and write "to".
Cut "due to the fact that" and write "because". Cut "at this point in time"
and write "now".

Rule 4. Never use the passive where you can use the active. Passive voice
buries the actor and diffuses responsibility. "The decision was made" hides
who made it. "We decided" does not.

Rule 5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon word if you can
think of an everyday English equivalent. Jargon signals in-group membership,
not intelligence. Plain words are a sign of confidence, not ignorance.

Rule 6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.
These are tools, not cages. A sentence fragment in the right place is better
than a grammatically correct sentence that deadens.

Orwell's diagnosis of bad writing has one root cause: the writer is not seeing
a mental picture of what they are describing. They are stringing together
ready-made phrases. Phrases tacked together "like the sections of a prefabricated
henhouse." The fix is always to think first, then write. Not to reach for the
nearest available phrase.


### Pinker (2014): The Sense of Style

Steven Pinker's contribution is the cognitive science explanation for why
experts write badly: the Curse of Knowledge. Once you know something, it becomes
nearly impossible to imagine what it is like not to know it. You write for
yourself, not for your reader.

The antidote Pinker calls "classic style". In classic style, the writer's job
is to show the reader something true and interesting, as clearly as possible.
Not to demonstrate expertise. Not to hedge against being wrong. Not to perform
neutrality. To show.

Key principles from Pinker's framework:

Treat writing as a guided tour, not a lecture. The reader is intelligent but
does not yet know what you know. Your job is to take them somewhere specific.

Use concrete examples, not abstract summaries. "The rule is important" tells
the reader nothing. "The rule saved us from a three-month contract dispute"
shows them something.

Old before new in every sentence. Each sentence should begin with something
the reader already has in mind and then introduce something new. This creates
flow. Reversing it creates friction.

Cut nominalizations (zombie nouns). Nominalizations take live verbs and kill
them by turning them into nouns. "The investigation of the problem" becomes
"investigating the problem". "The committee's evaluation of the proposal"
becomes "the committee evaluated the proposal". Every nominalization adds
length and saps life from prose.

Common nominalizations to eliminate:
- "make an assumption" - assume
- "provide a solution" - solve
- "reach a conclusion" - conclude
- "give consideration to" - consider
- "have an impact on" - affect
- "make a recommendation" - recommend
- "conduct an analysis" - analyse
- "take action" - act
- "have knowledge of" - know
- "perform a review" - review


### Williams (1981): Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace

Joseph Williams taught at the University of Chicago for decades. His core
principle is simple: clear sentences put characters in subject position and
actions in verb position.

Characters + Actions = Clarity.

Bad: "The failure of the system to process the request resulted in a
disruption of operations."

Good: "The system failed to process the request and disrupted operations."

The first sentence has buried characters (system, operations) inside noun
phrases. The second puts them in subject and object position, where the
reader's brain expects them.

Williams also defined the old-before-new principle that Pinker later popularised:
every sentence should link backward before moving forward. The last important
word in one sentence should connect to the first important idea of the next.
This creates cohesion. Its absence creates the experience of jumping.

His rules for concision:

Delete empty openers. "There is growing evidence that..." becomes "Evidence
suggests...". "It is the case that..." gets cut entirely.

Delete meaningless hedges. "Rather", "quite", "somewhat", "various", "certain"
(when used as filler): cut them. They weaken every sentence they enter.

Delete redundant words. "Past history" is just "history". "Future plans" is
just "plans". "Unexpected surprise" is just "surprise". "Complete stop" is
just "stop".

Combine sentences to eliminate unnecessary words. Two short sentences often
hide a relationship that a single cleaner sentence would show.


### Strunk and White (1959): The Elements of Style

Strunk and White's contribution is the most famous rule in English prose:
omit needless words.

"Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words,
a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing
should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."

Their most important specific rules:

Prefer the active voice. Use it in at least 80% of sentences.

Use definite, specific, concrete language. "A period of unfavorable weather
set in" is weaker than "It rained every day for a week."

Avoid fancy words. The writer who uses "utilize" instead of "use" is not more
precise. They are showing off.

Do not overstate. One forceful statement is worth a hundred weak ones.
Overstatement trains readers to distrust everything.

Place the emphatic word at the end of the sentence. The last word carries the
most weight. "He was a writer of great power and elegance" lands worse than
"He wrote with power and elegance."


### The Readability Research

Flesch's readability formula (1948) and the decades of research that followed
it established that readers process shorter sentences and common words faster
with higher comprehension. The average English sentence in formal prose should
run 15 to 20 words. Anything consistently above 25 words tests patience. The
occasional very short sentence (under 8 words) adds emphasis and rest.

The research also showed that syllable count predicts difficulty. One-syllable
words land faster than three-syllable ones. "Help" over "assistance". "Start"
over "initiate". "Use" over "utilise". "End" over "terminate".


## AI Writing Patterns to Avoid

This section covers the detection side. The scraper in
`scripts/update_patterns.py` updates `references/live_patterns.md` daily with
new intelligence. Read that file for the most current patterns.

The core patterns are stable and belong here.


### Banned Vocabulary

Tier 1 (never use, they are documented AI fingerprints):
delve, tapestry, underscore, pivotal, landscape, foster, testament, enhance,
intricate, robust, innovative, transformative, groundbreaking, realm, adept,
prowess, harness, illuminate, facilitate, bolster, palpable, navigate,
showcase, elevate, leverage (as verb), empower

Tier 2 (avoid strongly, high frequency in AI output):
nuance, beacon, cacophony, ever-evolving, cutting-edge, crucial, vital,
seamlessly, holistic, synergy, comprehensive, dynamic, paradigm, ecosystem,
cornerstone, linchpin, bedrock

Tier 3 (crutch phrases to cut entirely):
"let's unpack", "no fluff", "at the end of the day", "it goes without saying",
"it is important to note that", "generally speaking", "to some extent",
"from a broader perspective", "in today's ever-changing landscape",
"in conclusion", "overall", "to summarize", "in summary",
"it is worth noting", "needless to say", "a testament to",
"at its core", "the power of", "game-changing", "north star",
"move the needle", "at the forefront"

Substitution principle (from Orwell Rule 2):
Do not replace a banned word with a fancier synonym. Replace it with the
specific, concrete thing the vague word was trying to say.

"landscape" -- name the actual market, sector, or situation
"delve into" -- look at, examine, get into, dig into
"testament to" -- proof of, shows that, demonstrates
"foster" -- build, grow, support, encourage (pick the right one)
"navigate" -- handle, deal with, work through
"robust" -- strong, solid, well-built (or describe the actual quality)


### Structural AI Patterns

Negative parallelism. "It is not X, it is Y." State the positive directly.
Drop the phantom counterargument.

Rule of threes. Every list has exactly three items. Break it. Use two, four,
or one depending on what is actually there.

False range. "From intimate gatherings to global movements." Two loosely
related things dressed as a spectrum. Say the actual things.

Misconception framing. Writing as though clearing up a belief the reader did
not hold. "Not only X, but also Y." State the claim without the phantom objection.

Compulsive summary. "In conclusion," "Overall," "To summarize." End on the
last real point. Never restate what was just said.

Participial phrase overuse. Main clause + comma + -ing phrase. Research shows
AI uses this at two to five times the human rate. Split into two sentences
or restructure.

Compulsive balance. "While there are certainly benefits, it is important to
consider the drawbacks." Take a position. Hedge only when the uncertainty is
genuine and specific.

Template architecture. Broad intro + thesis + three body paragraphs + summary
conclusion. Break the template. Start with the most interesting thing.


### Punctuation

No em dash where a comma, colon, full stop, or parentheses works. Use em
dashes rarely and only for deliberate emphasis.

Use en dashes for ranges: dates, scores, page numbers.

No excessive mid-prose bolding. Bold for navigation in long reference
documents, not for decoration in prose.

No emoji in headers or body text unless the context specifically calls for it.


### The Physics of Human Writing

Perplexity: human writers choose words for reasons a language model does not
optimize for, including memory, rhythm, specificity, humour, and context
only the writer carries. This produces word choices a model would not predict.

Burstiness: human writing varies sentence length aggressively. A three-word
sentence. Then one that builds momentum and drives toward a resolution across
a full breath's worth of words before it closes. Then short again.

Both perplexity and burstiness must be present. Varying sentence length with
predictable vocabulary still reads as generated. The specific, unexpected,
accurate word is the other half of the solution.
