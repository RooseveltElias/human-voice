## Source 1: George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)

Published in the journal Horizon. Reprinted 118 times across 325 editions of
58 writing readers between 1946 and 1996. One of the most cited essays in
writing instruction.

### Orwell's Diagnosis of Bad Writing

Orwell identified four categories of writing failure:

**Dying metaphors.** Worn-out phrases used without awareness of their original
meaning. They do not evoke images. They fill space. His examples: "iron
resolution", "stand shoulder to shoulder with", "fishing in troubled waters",
"hotbed", "swan song". The test: can you picture the image? If not, the phrase
is dead.

**Verbal false limbs.** Phrases that eliminate simple verbs by turning them
into noun constructions. "Render inoperative" instead of "break". "Make
contact with" instead of "meet". "Play a leading role in" instead of "lead".
"Give grounds for" instead of "justify". These add syllables that give an
appearance of depth while removing action.

**Pretentious diction.** Latin and Greek roots used to dress up simple
statements. "Phenomenon" for "thing". "Objective" for "aim". "Utilize" for
"use". "Exhibit" for "show". "Facilitate" for "help". "Eliminate" for "end".
The writer who reaches for the Latin root is not being more precise. They
are hiding behind it.

**Meaningless words.** Abstract words with no fixed referent: "values",
"natural", "vitality", "democratic", "progressive". These can mean almost
anything, which means they mean almost nothing.

### Orwell's Key Principle

"The whole tendency of modern prose is away from concreteness."

The root failure is not grammatical. It is that the writer is not seeing a
mental picture of what they are describing. They are reaching for ready-made
phrases. These phrases "will construct your sentences for you, even think
your thoughts for you, to a certain extent, and at need they will perform the
important service of partially concealing your meaning even from yourself."

The fix: think first in pictures and sensations. Then hunt for words that fit
the picture. "Let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way around."

### Orwell's Six Rules

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used
   to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can
   think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

### Orwell's Demonstration

He translated a verse from Ecclesiastes into the kind of prose he criticised:

Original (49 words, 60 syllables, all everyday words, six vivid images):
"I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the
battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of
understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth
to them all."

Modern version (38 words, 90 syllables, 18 from Latin or Greek, zero fresh images):
"Objective considerations of contemporary phenomena compel the conclusion that
success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be
commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the
unpredictable must invariably be taken into account."

The second sentence gives only a shortened version of the meaning contained
in the first, using twice the syllable count. This is the trade the writer
makes when they reach for Latin abstractions instead of concrete English.

## Source 2: E.B. White and William Strunk Jr., "The Elements of Style" (1959)

First published as Strunk's private manual in 1918. White revised and expanded
it in 1959. It remains the most widely sold English style guide.

### Strunk's Core Rule

"Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words,
a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should
have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not
that the writer make all sentences short, or avoid all detail and treat subjects
only in outline, but that every word tell."

### Key Principles from Strunk and White

Place yourself in the background. The writer's personality should emerge from
the writing, not be announced. Bad writers declare themselves stylish. Good
writers just write well.

Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete
to the abstract. "A period of unfavorable weather set in" is weaker than "It
rained every day for a week."

Use active voice. Use it in at least 80% of sentences. The sentence "The
dog bit the man" is stronger and cleaner than "The man was bitten by the dog."

Do not overstate. "Overstating is as bad as understating. The reader will
trust the writer who does not claim too much and will distrust the one who
claims too much."

Use definite language. "Avoid the use of qualifiers. Rather, very, little,
pretty. These are the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood
of words."

Place the emphatic word at the end of the sentence. The last position in a
sentence carries the most stress. Design sentences so the most important word
lands there.

## Source 3: Joseph M. Williams, "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace" (1981)

Williams taught the "Little Red Schoolhouse" writing course at the University
of Chicago. This book is based on that course. It went through twelve editions
and became a standard text across American universities.

### The Core Formula

Characters + Actions = Clear sentences.

"Two principles define a clear sentence. First, its subjects should be the
main characters in the story. Second, those subjects should be followed by
verbs that name their key actions."

When subjects name abstract concepts and verbs name states of being rather
than actions, sentences become unclear not because of difficult words but
because of structure.

### The Nominalization Problem

A nominalization is a verb or adjective turned into a noun. Nominalizations
are the most common source of unclear prose in professional writing.

Examples of verb-to-noun conversions (nominalizations):
- "fail" becomes "the failure of"
- "decide" becomes "make a decision"
- "assume" becomes "make an assumption"
- "recommend" becomes "provide a recommendation"
- "analyze" becomes "conduct an analysis"
- "conclude" becomes "reach a conclusion"
- "solve" becomes "provide a solution"
- "improve" becomes "achieve an improvement"

Each conversion adds words and removes energy. "The committee decided" (4 words)
becomes "The committee made a decision" (6 words). The longer version is not
more precise. It is heavier.

The diagnostic test: if a sentence has more than two nominalizations, rewrite
it. Start with the character and put the action back into a verb.

### Old Before New

Each sentence should begin with something the reader already knows or has been
thinking about, and end with something new. This is the principle of cohesion.

"Readers understand new information more easily when it appears at the end of
a sentence, after the context that introduces and explains it."

When new information appears at the start and familiar information at the end,
readers feel they are constantly starting over. This is the experience of bad
prose. It is not the content that confuses. It is the order.

### Williams on Concision

Delete empty openers:
- "There is a reason that..." becomes "The reason is..."
- "It is important to recognise that..." disappears entirely
- "What I want to argue here is that..." becomes the argument itself

Delete meaningless modifiers:
- "various" (unless you mean it specifically): cut
- "certain" (as a vague qualifier): cut
- "quite", "rather", "somewhat", "fairly": cut almost always
- "basically", "essentially": cut almost always

Delete redundancies:
- "past history" -- "history"
- "future plans" -- "plans"
- "unexpected surprise" -- "surprise"
- "first began" -- "began"
- "end result" -- "result"
- "collaborate together" -- "collaborate"
- "consensus of opinion" -- "consensus"
- "completely finished" -- "finished"

## Source 4: Steven Pinker, "The Sense of Style" (2014)

Pinker is a cognitive scientist at Harvard. He applies linguistics and
cognitive psychology to the question of why expert writers write badly and
what can be done about it.

### The Curse of Knowledge

The most important concept in the book. Once you know something well, it
becomes almost impossible to imagine what it is like not to know it. Experts
write for themselves, not for their readers, because they cannot access their
reader's ignorance.

"The form in which thoughts occur to a writer is rarely the same as the form
in which they can be absorbed by a reader."

The antidote is not to simplify content. It is to remember that the reader is
intelligent but uninformed. They can follow complexity if it is presented in
the right order with the right signposts.

### Classic Style

Pinker distinguishes several writing styles. The one he advocates is classic
style, derived from the work of Francis-Noel Thomas and Mark Turner.

In classic style, "the writer knows something, and the reader's job is to
see what the writer sees." The prose is like a clear window onto the world,
not a display of the writer's erudition or caution.

Classic style is direct. It uses the active voice, short sentences, and
everyday words. It trusts the reader to follow without constant hand-holding.
It does not hedge every claim. It asserts.

Classic style is not simple-minded. It can handle complex ideas. The complexity
is in the ideas, not the language used to convey them.

### Against Nominalizations

Pinker calls nominalizations "zombie nouns." They take live processes and
deadify them. "We need to have a discussion about the situation" becomes "We
need to discuss the situation" and then "We need to discuss this."

Every zombie noun is an opportunity to bring a sentence back to life. Find
the hidden verb and restore it.

### Signposting

Good writers help readers know where they are in the argument. But excessive
signposting ("now I will turn to the second argument", "as I noted above",
"having established X, I will now consider Y") reads as bureaucratic and
signals that the structure is not doing the job on its own.

If the structure is clear, signposting is redundant. Write the structure first.
Add signposts only where the reader genuinely needs orientation.

## Source 5: Readability Research (Flesch, 1948; Gunning, 1952)

Rudolf Flesch's readability formula (1948) and Robert Gunning's Fog Index (1952)
established quantitative relationships between prose features and comprehension.

### Key Findings

Average sentence length predicts readability. For general professional prose,
15 to 20 words per sentence is the target range. Sentences above 30 words
consistently reduce comprehension speed and accuracy.

Syllable count predicts word-level difficulty. One-syllable words process
faster than polysyllabic ones. This is why "help" serves better than
"assistance", "use" better than "utilise", "start" better than "initiate".

Sentence length variation improves engagement. Monotonous sentence length, even
at the right average, reduces reader attention. The research supports aggressive
alternation between short and long.

The best professional prose mixes sentence lengths with an average in the 15
to 20 word range, uses predominantly common words, and contains no sentence
above 35 words more than once per page.

## Applications to AI Writing Detection

The principles above explain why AI-generated prose fails at a structural level,
not just a vocabulary level.

AI produces low perplexity (predictable words) because models optimise for the
most statistically likely next token. Orwell's Rule 1 and 2 are the antidote:
use specific, common, original words. Not the safe, elevated, expected ones.

AI produces low burstiness (uniform sentence length) because the training
distribution of professional text skews toward medium-length sentences.
Strunk's rule on concision and the readability research both push toward
shorter average length with high variance.

AI produces nominalizations at high rates because nominalizations are
extremely common in the academic and professional text that makes up training
data. Williams's character-and-action framework is the antidote.

AI defaults to compulsive balance ("while there are benefits, there are also
drawbacks") because training on debate-format and essay-format text rewards
apparent fairness. Pinker's classic style framework is the antidote: state
what you see, clearly, and trust the reader.

AI produces template architecture (intro, three body paragraphs, conclusion)
because this structure saturates training data through student essays, how-to
articles, and corporate writing. Breaking the template at the structural level,
not just the word level, is required.
